About

I’m a computer engineer at heart. I’ve worked as a full-stack software engineer, AI engineer, data engineer and data scientist, often all on the same project. What ties these roles together is one goal: taking an idea from its very first spark to something people actually use.

My story started with a love of building things. During my Computer Engineering degree at the University of Peradeniya, the projects that excited me most were the ones that had to work outside the classroom. One was a smart greenhouse system that used cameras and sensors to keep watch over crops. It won first place at HACKFEST and first runner-up at IEEE WIE’s Code With WIE. More importantly, it taught me that a clever idea is only worth something when the system around it works reliably.

I carried that lesson into Rozie AI, where I worked remotely with a US-based machine learning team. There, most problems that reached me began as two things: a large pile of messy text and a business question. My job was to connect them. I built systems that turned thousands of customer conversations into clear insights, made an AI search tool give more accurate answers, and created ways to check whether AI results could truly be trusted. Along the way, I learned to work out what the data can honestly answer, build the system that answers it, and explain the result to people who will never read the code.

My curiosity doesn’t stop when the workday ends. I built CareFlow, a booking and queue system for medical clinics, and learned what it means to create software that real patients and staff depend on. I also built an ATM Cash Optimizer that predicts how much cash each machine will need and plans refills around it. When tested on real ATM data, it cut cash-handling costs by 35–42%.

The more AI systems I built, the more I wanted to understand why they behave the way they do. That question led me to research. I presented my first paper, on a low-cost AI tutor for students, as first author at the IEEE ICEEET 2026 conference. Today, I’m studying for an MSc in Data Science and AI at the University of Moratuwa, and my research explores how AI assistants can protect people’s private conversations.

Long before any of this, I grew up on the badminton court. I played at national level in school tournaments, represented Sri Lanka at an international tournament in Singapore, and later captained the University of Peradeniya women’s team. The court taught me that matches are rarely won by one brilliant shot. They’re won through patience, preparation and staying steady through the long rallies, and I’ve found that building good software works the same way. Years of competition taught me how to prepare, take feedback and lead a team, and I bring those same habits to engineering.

Now I’m looking for a long-term role with a team that builds real products and cares about getting them right. I want to keep doing what I love most: taking a messy, open-ended problem, finding what the data can really say, and turning it into something people rely on every day.

How I think

Every project I take on follows the same path, and each step builds on the last.

  1. 01
    stakeholders → requirements → success metrics

    Before I write any code, I talk with the stakeholders who will use the result. I want to know what decision they need to make and which success metrics will show it works. Their answers become the requirements that guide every step after.

  2. 02
    data profiling → data quality → edge cases

    With the requirements clear, I explore the data. I check its quality, look for gaps, bias and edge cases, and learn what it can and cannot answer. Finding problems here is much easier than fixing them in production.

  3. 03
    Pipelines → models → APIs

    Once I trust the data, I start building. A model is only one piece. People need something they can use every day, so I build everything around it too, from the pipelines that bring the data in to the APIs, cloud infrastructure and dashboards they see.

  4. 04
    evaluation → monitoring → iteration

    When the system is live, I make sure it gives the right answers. I evaluate it against ground truth and real baselines, monitor it for drift after launch, and fix what goes wrong. What I learn goes back to step one, so each version is better than the last.
